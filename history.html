The Spectrum story

A computer built to a price by a man who cared more about electric cars, sold in its millions, coloured in 8×8 squares, and copied chip for chip in Brazil. It is the machine K-Prompt emulates, along with Microdigital's TK90X and TK95.

1Sir Clive

Clive Sinclair (1940–2021) left school at 18 and sold miniature electronics kits to hobbyists by post. His company, Sinclair Radionics, made the Sinclair Executive in 1972, "the world's first slimline electronic pocket calculator", and then the Black Watch, a digital watch that was inaccurate and hard to service.

Computers came next and sold by the hundred thousand. The ZX80 (1980) cost £99.95 built. The ZX81 cost £69.95 built, or £49.95 as a kit, and sold more than 1.5 million. The Spectrum (1982) sold over five million. He was knighted in 1983 "for his contributions to the personal computer industry", and he chaired Mensa from 1980 to 1997.

His heart was elsewhere. Jimmy Maher writes that he "really didn't know that much about computers, nor did he care all that much about them". The computers paid for his pocket televisions and his electric vehicle. The C5, a one-seat electric tricycle with a top speed of 15 mph, was launched on 10 January 1985 at a snowy Alexandra Palace, built by Hoover in a washing-machine factory, and was in receivership by October. About 14,000 were made, and sources disagree on how few were sold. The QL, unveiled in January 1984, was promised within 28 days before a working one existed.

On 7 April 1986 Sinclair Research sold its computers and the Sinclair name to Amstrad for £5 million. Sir Clive went on with folding bikes (the A-bike), an electric car that never shipped (the X-1), televised poker, and in 2014 a warning that thinking machines would make it "very difficult for us to survive". In 2010 he said he did not use computers.

The last Sinclair-designed Spectrum was the 128, made for the Spanish distributor Investrónica and shown in Spain in September 1985, in Britain in February 1986. It added a second ROM with a menu and a full-screen BASIC editor, eight 16K banks of RAM paged in at C000h, and an AY-3-8912 sound chip with three voices (128 BASIC plays it with PLAY). Amstrad's +2 (1986) put the same machine in a grey case with a proper keyboard and a cassette recorder built in. K-Prompt runs both, menu, banks, AY and all.

The fight at the Baron of Beef

Chris Curry had worked for Sinclair before founding Acorn, maker of the BBC Micro. In December 1984, the story goes, Sinclair met him in a Cambridge pub, the Baron of Beef. Angry at Acorn adverts that called Sinclair machines shoddy, he hit Curry about the head with a rolled-up newspaper. There was pushing but no punches, and it went on later at a wine bar.

A newspaper columnist who was there wrote it up for Christmas Eve, and the tabloids made it a punch-up. Accounts differ on the columnist's name and the cause, and one Acorn veteran did not remember it at all. The BBC made it a scene in Micro Men (2009).

2Built to be cheap

The Spectrum was launched on 23 April 1982 at £125 for 16K and £175 for 48K, cut to £99 and £129 the next year. A Commodore 64 or a BBC Model B cost over £300. Everything about it served that price.

One custom chipRichard Altwasser put the video, keyboard, sound and tape logic into a single Ferranti ULA (chapter 3). Beside it: a Z80A at 3.5 MHz, a 16K ROM, the RAM, and very little else.
Half-bad memoryThe 48K's upper 32K is eight 64K chips with one faulty half, sold cheaply as 32K parts (TI TMS4532, OKI MSM3732). A link on the board picks which half works; all eight must agree.
Rubber keys40 rubber keys over a membrane, likened to "dead flesh". One reviewer wrote that "you need to squeeze the keys rather than press them". Hard keys only came with the Spectrum+ in 1984.
Keywords on the keysSteve Vickers's 16K ROM holds a whole BASIC. Each keyword is one keypress and one byte (P is PRINT), which saved memory and typing; the cursor's K means "a keyword comes next".
A beeperNo sound chip: one bit of port FEh drives a small speaker, and the CPU makes every sound by toggling it at the right moments. The manual promised ten octaves.
Your TV and your tapeThe picture went to a home TV through an RF lead and programs to an ordinary cassette recorder, at about 1,500 baud.
The rainbowRick Dickinson's black case, with a rainbow stripe across one corner. Thirty-five years later he designed the Next's case too.
Made by TimexThe machines were assembled by Timex in Dundee, which also led to the American Timex Sinclairs (chapter 8).

Altwasser and Vickers left soon after the launch to found Jupiter Cantab, whose Jupiter Ace ran Forth instead of BASIC. One name suggested for the Spectrum was "Not the BBC Micro".

3Ferranti and the ULA

A ULA, an uncommitted logic array, is a gate array. The chip is made in advance with transistors that do nothing yet, and a customer's design is added as the final metal wiring layers. That is far cheaper than a fully custom chip. Ferranti, the British electronics firm, pioneered bipolar ULAs. Its first notable customer was a Rollei camera in 1972, and its arrays went into the ZX81, the Spectrum, the BBC Micro and the Acorn Electron.

The Spectrum's ULA does almost everything that is not the CPU. It reads the screen from RAM and turns it into a TV picture, 50 times a second. It raises the Z80's interrupt at the start of each frame. On I/O port FEh it reads the keyboard, sets the border colour, drives the beeper, and reads and writes the cassette.

The screen RAM is shared. While the ULA reads it, a CPU access to 4000h–7FFFh has to wait, in the pattern 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1, 0, 0 T-states repeating every 8 T-states across each line of the picture. This is "contention", and programs that count cycles (border effects, multicolour, some loaders) depend on it. When the Z80's I register points into that memory, its refresh cycles upset the ULA's fetches and the screen fills with "snow", though the machine keeps running.

In 2010 Chris Smith published The ZX Spectrum ULA: How to Design a Microcomputer, the result of reverse-engineering the chip. Its account of the timing, such as the interrupt being held for exactly 32 T-states, is what emulators now rely on.

Spectrum ULAs and their fixes

  • 5C102E: Issue 1 boards. Its I/O contention made machine-code keyboard reads unreliable; the fix was the "dead cockroach", a 74LS00 chip mounted upside down on a little board.
  • 5C112E: fixed that, but contended ports that should not be; the fix was the "spider", a transistor, later TR6 on Issue 3 boards.
  • 6C001E-5, -6, -7: Ferranti's lower-power process, through Issue 3. The -7 is the one documented to work on every 48K issue; late ones say Plessey, which bought Ferranti's chip business in 1988.
  • ZX81: the 2C158E, Sinclair's first ULA.

4Colour clash

The screen is 256×192 pixels, but colour comes in 8×8 cells. The bitmap takes 6,144 bytes, one bit per pixel. Then 768 bytes give each cell one attribute: an ink colour, a paper colour (eight each), BRIGHT, and FLASH, which swaps ink and paper every 0.64 s. Colour for every pixel would have cost several kilobytes more, on a 16K machine.

So a cell can hold two colours. When a yellow sprite walks over a green tree, the tree's cells turn yellow or the sprite turns green: "large ugly fringes of rapidly changing colours". Games answered in several ways: colour only for borders and scores around a monochrome play area; two colours for everything, as in Ultimate's Knight Lore; very careful design, as in Light Force; or monochrome sprites over coloured backgrounds, as in Double Dragon.

The bitmap's odd layout (rows in thirds, lines interleaved) was decided under time pressure. It is why Spectrum screens load from tape in that famous venetian-blind order.

Cheating the ULA

The ULA reads the attributes again for each of a cell's 8 lines. A program timed to the T-state can change them between lines and get colour on every pixel row, over about 20 columns. Demo coders call it multicolour. Engines such as Nirvana and Bifrost*2 put it in games, decades after the machine was sold. ULA.tap, on K-Prompt's shelf, shows it beside plain colour clash.

Clash and the beeper became the look and sound of the platform, in the same way the C64 is remembered for its sprites and SID.

5Bedroom coders and the fans

A cheap machine with BASIC in ROM and a tape recorder to sell games on started the British games industry. Matthew Smith wrote Manic Miner at 17. Tim and Chris Stamper's Ultimate Play the Game (Jetpac, Knight Lore) became Rare. Ocean in Manchester began as mail-order "Spectrum Games". The Darling brothers founded Codemasters in 1986 for budget games "with the quality of full-priced games".

Magazines carried the scene: Sinclair User (1982–93), Crash (1984–92) with Oliver Frey's painted covers, and Your Sinclair (1984–93, born as Your Spectrum), known for its jokes. Programs were printed as listings to type in, for hours, and errors were corrected in the next issue.

The fans call it the Speccy, and they never left. World of Spectrum (1995) set out to be "a completely free museum" of its software. Spectrum Computing and its open ZXDB database still add new titles, since new games are written and sold on tape every year. The playground argument with Commodore 64 owners has never quite ended.

The ZX Spectrum Next

A Spectrum rebuilt in an FPGA, compatible with the old software and much more. It began in Brazil in 2010 as TBBlue, a variant of the TK95, by Victor Trucco and Fábio Belavenuto, the same pair who cloned the TK90X's ULA (chapter 6). Henrique Olifiers took it to the UK. Rick Dickinson designed the case and died during development. Its 2017 Kickstarter raised £723,390, and later ones more.

6Brazil: Microdigital's TK90X and TK95

From 1979, Brazil reserved its computer market for national companies through the SEI, the Special Secretariat for Informatics. Lei 7.232 of October 1984 made the policy law (the same reserva de mercado that shaped Brazil's MSXs, told on Ok-Prompt's page). Foreign computers could not be imported, but they could be copied.

Microdigital Eletrônica was founded in São Paulo in 1981 by the brothers Tomas and Jorge (George) Kovari; "TK" is their initials. Its first machines copied Sinclair's: the TK80 (a ZX80), then the TK82C and TK85 (ZX81s). Those had no ULA. A dozen TTL chips did its work, so they ran warmer. In 1983 Sinclair Research sued Microdigital in Brazil for copyright infringement and lost: the court treated the ROM as part of the hardware, not as protected software. Brazil's software law only came in 1987.

The TK90X followed in June 1985: a 48K Spectrum in a slightly taller case, with 40 rubber keys, a built-in joystick port on an Atari connector, and sound through the TV instead of a speaker. It sold about 2,500 a month in late 1986. The TK95 (1986) put the same machine in a case borrowed from the Commodore Plus/4, with a 57-key keyboard. Its introductory price was Cz$ 4,559. Brazil's MSXs, the Expert and the Hotbit, overshadowed both, and Microdigital was gone by 1990.

Cloning the ULA

This time Microdigital made a chip. The TK90X's ULA, marked MC16845, is its own design. Wikipedia says it was reverse-engineered as CMOS from Ferranti's bipolar original. It fits the same socket pin for pin, with one addition: pin 1 selects 50 or 60 Hz. Former Microdigital engineer Cláudio Cassens says the "national ULA" took about a year. Who fabricated it is not known: the documentation "seems to be irretrievably lost".

It is not an exact copy. It runs from a 14.3 MHz crystal (four times Brazil's colour subcarrier), so the Z80 runs at 3.5756 MHz, and every line is 228 T-states, not 224. Brazilian TVs are PAL-M, 60 Hz, so a TK90X draws 262 lines 59.86 times a second (chapter 7). Some TK90Xs built for Argentina carry genuine Ferranti ULAs, one dated January 1985, and how Microdigital got them is a mystery.

In 2012–13 Victor Trucco and Fábio Belavenuto cloned the Microdigital ULA in a CPLD, "the component that until then was irreplaceable", and published it as open hardware.

8America: Timex Sinclair

Timex, which built Sinclair's machines in Dundee, sold them in the United States as a joint venture. The TS1000 (July 1982) was a ZX81 with 2K, "the first home computer to cost under $100", and sold about 550,000 that year. The TS1500 (1983) was a 16K ZX81 in a Spectrum-style case.

The TS2068 (November 1983, $199) was the American Spectrum, with more than a new name. It had an AY-3-8912 sound chip, two joystick ports, a cartridge slot, a 24K ROM, and an SCLD chip in place of the ULA with extra screen modes (dual screen, 512-pixel high resolution). The price of all that was compatibility: fewer than one in ten Spectrum machine-code programs ran on it. Timex left the US market in early 1984, but Timex Portugal carried on with the TC 2048 and TC 2068.

K-Prompt runs the TS2068 on its own ROMs (the 16K home ROM and the 8K EXROM, which holds the tape routines): the SCLD's 60 Hz frame of 262 lines, its screen modes (the second screen at 6000h, "hi-colour" with an attribute for every 8×1 pixels, and 512 pixels across in two colours, drawn at double width), the memory map that swaps the EXROM into any 8K of the 64K, the AY at ports F5h and F6h, and its joystick, read through the AY. The cartridge dock is empty.

9How K-Prompt emulates the ULA

K-Prompt has no ULA chip to copy. It reproduces what the ULA does, at the moments it does it, counted in the Z80's T-states.

The frameEach frame is 69,888 T-states of Z80 time (59,736 on a TK at 60 Hz, 70,908 on a 128K). For the first 32 (36 on a 128K) the interrupt line is low, and the CPU takes it if it is listening, as on the real machine. Then the program runs until the frame is spent.
The pictureEach 8-pixel cell is read from memory at the T-state the beam reaches it, so a program that changes the screen mid-line is seen exactly where it acts. The bitmap's thirds and interleaved lines are decoded as the ULA fetches them.
The borderEvery OUT to port FEh is kept with its T-state. Each border pixel takes the colour in force when the beam passed it, so loading stripes and border effects land where they did on a TV.
FLASHInk and paper swap every 16 frames, 0.64 s on a 48K.
The keyboardReading port FEh, the high address byte picks half-rows of keys, and several half-rows AND together, so games that scan the whole keyboard at once work.
The beeperEAR and MIC changes are noted with their T-state. Each sound sample is the average level over its slice of the frame, which keeps the square waves clean.
The tapeStandard blocks are copied in at once by catching the ROM's LD-BYTES. Others play in real time as pulses on the EAR bit: the ROM, or a turbo loader, decodes them itself and the border stripes.
ContentionWhile the ULA reads the display, 128 T-states of every line, it holds the CPU off 4000h–7FFFh. An access there waits for its free slot, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1, 0 or 0 T-states depending on where in the ULA's 8-T-state cycle it falls; the ULA's own ports wait too. K-Prompt's Z80 times every memory and I/O cycle on its own, so each one waits exactly as long as on a real 48K (checked against FUSE's tests, cycle by cycle). The TK uses the same pattern from its own first pixel, as ESPectrum does.
The floating busReading a port nothing answers returns whatever is on the data bus: during the display, the bitmap or attribute byte the ULA is fetching at that T-state; elsewhere FFh. Games such as Arkanoid and Sidewize sync to the beam this way. Port 1Fh floats too when no Kempston interface is plugged in.

10The siblings

K-Prompt is the third of a family, each named after the prompt its machine waits at, and all on the same Z80 core. A-Prompt runs CP/M 2.2 and its A>. Ok-Prompt is an MSX, which answers Ok, as the TK90X does. All three have a telnet modem that dials BBSes.
